Games

  • Jugglin

    Jugglin is a standard-deck Tarock game for three or four, where every tarock has a twin and the Pagat beats the Fool.

    3–4 players · 40–60 min · teach in 15 min

    You need One standard deck with Jokers

    Overview

    3 players

    Jugglin is a point-trick game for three with a standard deck of 52 cards and its two Jokers. The Jokers, the Aces, and the 2s to 5s are the tarocks, which are permanent trumps.

    Players bid on how many talon cards they want. The fewer cards, the higher the bid. The auction winner becomes the Declarer and plays alone against the other two players, the Defenders. In a Point game, the Declarer needs 41 of the 80 card points. In Tightrope, they may win at most one trick. If nobody bids, everyone plays Ramsch alone.

    The deck holds two Fools, two Monds, and two Pagats, the Honours of the game. A Pagat, normally the lowest tarock, beats a Fool whenever they meet in a trick. This is the Juggler’s Trick.

    Every deal is played for chips. In a Point game, certain moments also pay immediately to the winner of a trick, such as a King meeting its Queen or one Honour capturing another. After three rounds, the player with the most chips wins.

    4 players

    Jugglin is a point-trick game for four with a standard deck of 52 cards and its two Jokers. The Jokers, the Aces, and the 2s to 5s are the tarocks, which are permanent trumps.

    Players bid on how many talon cards they want. The fewer cards, the higher the bid. The auction winner becomes the Declarer. In a Point game, the Declarer calls a King, and whoever holds it becomes their secret partner. Together they need 39 of the 76 card points against the other two players, the Defenders. By calling a King they hold themselves, the Declarer plays alone against all three, and nobody knows it until that King is played. If nobody bids, everyone plays Ramsch alone.

    The deck holds two Fools, two Monds, and two Pagats, the Honours of the game. A Pagat, normally the lowest tarock, beats a Fool whenever they meet in a trick. This is the Juggler’s Trick.

    Every deal is played for chips. In a Point game, certain moments also pay immediately to the winner of a trick, such as a King meeting its Queen or one Honour capturing another. After the last round, the player with the most chips wins.

    Differences

    Both versions use the same deck, card ranking, Juggler’s Trick, bids and moments.

    In 3p, the Declarer always plays alone against the other two. Besides the Point game, there’s Tightrope, where the Declarer may win at most one trick.

    In 4p, the Declarer calls a King, and whoever holds it becomes their secret partner. Nobody knows the sides until that King hits the table. Once the partners are known, both sides exchange a card. If you call your own King, you play alone against the other three.

    Ramsch also plays differently. In 3p, the player with the most card points pays 3 chips to each of the others, so you just need to avoid taking the most. In 4p, each player pays 2 chips to every player with fewer card points.

    Designer’s note

    Wow. This game makes you feel things, good and bad. It has a serious core, but then there’s also lots of laughter and Schadenfreude. The 3p version is a bit more strategic, the 4p version a bit more exciting IMO. Obviously, this Tarock game is played with a regular deck of playing cards. Through this, there are two copies of each tarock. Why be happy with one Fool when you could have two Fools? Unfortunately, the Pagats (the lowest tarocks) are now able to beat them. And if it happens, it hurts no matter if you’ll win the contract or not. Ouch.

  • Peelin

    Peelin is a shedding duel for two, made for the Trifle deck but playable with a regular one. Stake a card on going out, peel your hand, and free your Queens.

    2 players · 10–30 min · teach in 5 min

    You need A Trifle deck, or 28 cards from a standard deck

    Overview

    Peelin is a shedding game for two. You may never rearrange your hand. You lead only from its left or right side, but you may beat with cards from anywhere. The Queens are the strongest cards, but a Queen must reach a side before you can play it.

    When you pass, you may pick a card from your opponent’s combination and put it anywhere into your hand.

    Before play, both players secretly stake one card on going out. A Queen is a big bet but leaves your hand weaker. A 0 is a safe bet and takes a weak card out of your hand.

    When a player goes out, they score points for every card left in their opponent’s hand and for both stakes. Each card is worth its rank, and a Queen is worth 10. The first player to reach the target score wins.

    Designer’s note

    A game that solely exists because I challenged myself to create a shedding game based on the Trifle deck. It’s very light, snappy, the scoring is painful and I enjoy the puzzle.

    It’s very different to Slamlin (aka waaay less complex) although I worked on both designs at the same time.

  • Slamlin

    Slamlin is a point-trick duel for two with handicap offers, hidden second cards, and a three-card lay-off.

    2 players · 60–90 min · teach in 20 min

    You need 32 cards from a standard deck (7 to Ace)

    Overview

    Slamlin is a point-trick game for two. Players compete to become the Declarer, who names the game type: a Trump, No-trump, or Nullo game.

    Instead of bidding points, you offer a harder target or handicaps. Handicaps give your opponent an edge, such as a pick from the talon or a power to use in the tricks. Targets and handicaps are worth levels. The more levels you offer, the more the contract is worth, and the more your opponent scores if you fail it. While the cards arrive, the higher offer earns a bonus. If you stay ahead, though, your opponent can pass and leave you to declare. If you win the auction, your opponent becomes the Defender and uses your handicaps against you.

    All 32 cards are in play. Once the talon is taken, you know exactly which cards your opponent holds. Two things stay hidden. Before the first trick, both players set aside three cards face down as a lay-off for the last three tricks. In the first six tricks, the double tricks, both players place their second card face down at the same time. Choosing the lay-off takes careful planning. Since you know each other’s cards, the second cards turn into bluffing and second-guessing.

    Both players score card points, so the Defender plays for points too. If the Defender, or in Nullo the Declarer, captures no more card points than the target allows, the Declarer makes the contract and scores its value. Otherwise, the Defender scores it, and the levels count double.

    After six deals, the player with the higher score wins. For your first match, see First Match.

    Designer’s note

    Slamlin is by far my most complex and thinky design. This one took quite some fine-tuning before I felt that it was finished. It’s a 2p trick-taker in which you declare, pick a game type, and try to win as many trick points as possible while the defender has some special powers to stop you.

    It’s meaty, I don’t expect that many people will have the time/energy/opponent to try it out, but the ones that do will be in for an unforgiving yet unique experience. At least that’s my hope.

  • Dopplin

    Dopplin is a two-player climbing and shedding game where players bid contracts and Aces shovel sets.

    2 players · 20–60 min · teach in 10 min

    You need Two standard decks with Jokers

    Overview

    Dopplin is a two-player climbing and shedding game played with a shortened double deck.

    Players lead and beat singles, pairs, sets, runs, and connected sets. There are no bombs. Instead, cards of the weakest rank can shovel an opponent’s pair or set into your hand and remain on the pile as the new active play.

    Before play begins, players bid for contracts that raise the stakes and set the terms of the hand: who leads, how set-aside cards enter play, and what the declarer must achieve. Victory comes from reading your hand, choosing the right contract, and knowing when to shed quickly, hold back, or shovel at the right moment.

    Designer’s note

    A shedder with contracts. You have strong cards? Good for you, but only if you can squeeze as many points out of them as possible. How? By making your life harder. The deck composition and combinations together with the shoveling turned out to be pretty fun.

    Usually, my goal when designing is to get it into favorite game territory with at least one person. This one turned out to become my favorite 2p game.

  • Grapplin

    Grapplin is a two-player climbing and shedding game where matching combinations grant special actions and jokers reverse rank order.

    2 players · 15–35 min · teach in 10 min

    You need One standard deck with Jokers

    Overview

    Playing Grapplin feels like a grappling match in which it is all about finding leverage in your opponent’s attack. Match one of their cards to control the exchange. Shift what you are countering by adding to their combination, break their structure by removing a card, or strengthen your position by pulling cards from the row and then attack back with full force. Caught in a bad hold? Flip your joker to reverse the rank order. Or catch your opponent off-guard by playing a bomb to create a high-pressure situation. First to shed all cards wins the round. First to three rounds wins.

    Designer’s note

    The one that started it all. It leans more on the hand management side with one important timing decision. Lots of agency and ways to express yourself.

    Is it a masterpiece? Probably not, but it’s my partner’s favorite game.

For other designers’ games

Variants and extras I made for other designers’ games.

  • The Maca Variant for Big A

    This variant grew over time, playing with the same group of friends, out of conversations we had about the game and a few personal quibbles.

    I find the base game too forgiving to the Declarer, especially the soloist. Now the Big A team needs a 1st place, and without one it usually hurts. Playing solo is also not as lucrative or as safe as it used to be. That’s fine by me, because I find defending against a soloist a bit boring, and whoever holds both Big Aces can now call a partner instead.

  • The Big Toad Variant for Phibo

    It makes Bryce Carter’s gem even more wild and chaotic by stripping out some rules. It’s turned into my favorite way of playing the game.

  • The Compendium Variant for The Blueberry Score

    Yeeeah I’m not an expert on compendium games but here it adds a bit more complexity for a bit more decision space. I created it on a whim and it hasn’t been playtested intensively. Still, it can be tried for a change of the base game.

  • Player board for Crosswhords! · PDF 168 KB

    I made a player board with a rules summary so I don’t have to draw a new grid every time I want to play or forget the word restrictions. Printed and laminated it, and it’s been a game-changer! If you’re playing Crosswhords! regularly, having a dedicated board saves time. Plus, having it on the shelf is a nice reminder to actually pull the game out more often. Sharing it here in case anyone else wants one with built-in rule reminders.

How I design

I love playing card games, especially obscure, innovative and traditional ones. I’d call what I do designing rather than inventing, because most of my ideas come from games like these. Sometimes it’s a game I keep coming back to, like Dalian Red 5 for Dopplin. Sometimes it’s a challenge: Peelin started with the question whether Sean Ross’s 28-card Trifle deck could carry a shedding game.

What drives me is trying things out, seeing potential in a game and making it more to my taste. Sometimes that means adding something new, but usually it means bringing two or more ideas together until the game feels like my own. I want skill and depth, but I also love a good laugh and some Schadenfreude at the table.

I strip away a lot, but not everything. Traditional card games have their bumps, and sometimes those are what give a game its character.

I also try to make rules and player aids as helpful as possible, so that getting started is easy.
