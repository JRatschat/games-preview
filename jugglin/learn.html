Learn Jugglin

Jugglin is a card game for three or four. Every trump has a twin, and the weakest trumps can beat the strongest. The winner of the bidding plays alone against the others, or, with four players, often with a secret partner. This page teaches it one idea at a time, as simply as possible.

The whole game in short

  1. Each player plays one card, and the best card wins all of them. This happens 16 times in a deal.
  2. Jokers, Aces and the 2s to 5s are strong cards that beat all the others. The two lowest of them, the red Aces, can beat the two highest, the Jokers.
  3. Before playing, you bid. With three players, the winner of the bidding plays alone against the other two and needs at least 41 of the 80 card points.
  4. Everything is paid in chips, some at once during play and the rest at the end of the deal. After 9 deals with three players, the player with the most chips wins.

What you need, and how this page works

  • Three or four players. This page teaches the game for three first.
  • One standard deck of 52 cards and its two Jokers.
  • Poker chips worth 1 and 5, about 200 chips’ worth for three players or 300 for four. Pen and paper work too (Part 4).

Part 1 (steps 0 to 6) is how cards are played. After step 6 you play a practice deal. Part 2 (steps 7 to 14) adds bidding, chips and the other ways to play a deal. Part 3 (steps 15 to 18) shows what changes with four players. Part 4 holds smaller rules. They matter in play, but you don’t need them while learning. If you forget what a word means, Part 4 also has a list of words.

Already know trick-taking games such as Skat or Tarock? Steps 3 and 4 will feel familiar. Read the others, because the tarocks, their order and the card points are Jugglin’s own. Then go on to step 7.

Part 1 Playing cards

0The cards

A deck has 4 suits, two red and two black.

  • ♥︎ Hearts, red
  • ♦︎ Diamonds, red
  • ♠︎ Spades, black
  • ♣︎ Clubs, black

Jugglin splits the 54 cards into two groups.

22 strong cards, the 2 Jokers and every Ace, 2, 3, 4 and 5JKA♠︎5♣︎4♥︎3♦︎2♣︎
32 suit cards, the King down to the 6 in each suitK♠︎Q♥︎10♦︎6♣︎

In each suit, the suit cards rank from King, the highest, down to 6.

K♠︎Q♠︎J♠︎10♠︎9♠︎8♠︎7♠︎6♠︎

An Ace or a card from 2 to 5 is never a suit card, even though it shows a suit.

The word The strong cards are tarocks. They are permanent trumps, so they beat every suit card.

Check yourself. Which of these are tarocks? 3♥︎, J♣︎, A♦︎, 6♠︎, Joker.

3♥︎, A♦︎ and the Joker. J♣︎ and 6♠︎ are suit cards.

1The order of the tarocks

Here are all 22 tarocks, from highest to lowest. Read them left to right, line by line.

JokersJKJK
Black AcesA♠︎A♣︎
Black 5s5♠︎5♣︎
Red 5s5♥︎5♦︎
Black 4s4♠︎4♣︎
Red 4s4♥︎4♦︎
Black 3s3♠︎3♣︎
Red 3s3♥︎3♦︎
Black 2s2♠︎2♣︎
Red 2s2♥︎2♦︎
Red AcesA♥︎A♦︎

So the Jokers are highest, then the black Aces, then the 5s down to the 2s, and the red Aces are lowest. Between two cards with the same number, the black one beats the red one. A red 5 still beats a black 4.

The words The Jokers are the Fools, the black Aces the Monds and the red Aces the Pagats. Together, these six cards are the Honours.

Check yourself. Which is higher, 3♦︎ or 3♠︎? 2♣︎ or 5♥︎? A♥︎ or 2♦︎?

3♠︎, because black beats red with the same number. 5♥︎, because a higher number beats any lower one. 2♦︎, because the red Aces are the lowest tarocks.

2Twins

Every tarock has a twin, a card with the same number and the same colour.

Twins5♣︎5♠︎
Twins5♥︎5♦︎
Not twins5♣︎5♥︎

The two Jokers are twins too, and so are the two black Aces and the two red Aces.

When both twins are played to the same trick (step 3), the one played first is the higher one.

Check yourself. One player plays 4♠︎, and the next player plays 4♣︎. Which is higher?

4♠︎. They are twins, and 4♠︎ was played first.

3Tricks, and following

Play goes clockwise. The player to the dealer’s left starts by playing any card. Then each other player adds one card. The three cards together are a trick.

You must follow the first card.

  1. If a suit card was led, play a card of that suit if you can.
  2. If you can’t, you must play a tarock if you have one.
  3. If you have neither, play any card.

If a tarock was led, you must play a tarock if you can.

The words Playing the first card of a trick is leading. The player to the dealer’s left is Forehand.

Check yourself. 9♥︎ is led. You hold 4♥︎, 8♣︎ and J♠︎. What must you play?

4♥︎. It shows a heart, but it is a tarock, so you have no hearts to follow with. Then you must play a tarock, and 4♥︎ is your only one.

4Who wins a trick

The highest tarock in the trick wins it. If there is no tarock, the highest card of the led suit wins. A card of another suit never wins.

9♥︎ is led. 2♦︎ wins, because it is a tarock.9♥︎K♥︎2♦︎
9♥︎ is led. K♥︎ wins. K♠︎ is the wrong suit.9♥︎K♥︎K♠︎

The winner takes the cards face down onto their pile and leads the next trick. You don’t have to beat the cards already played, except in Tightrope and Ramsch (step 12).

Check yourself. J♣︎ is led, then 6♣︎ and 5♦︎. Who wins?

The player of 5♦︎, because it is a tarock.

5The Juggler’s Trick

There is one exception to the order. When a Fool and a Pagat are in the same trick, the Pagat wins, whatever else is in the trick.

The lowest tarock beats the highestJKA♠︎A♥︎

With both Pagats in the trick, the one played first wins.

The word This is the Juggler’s Trick.

Check yourself. Who wins each trick? First, a Fool, 5♣︎ and 2♥︎. Second, A♦︎, 3♠︎ and 4♥︎.

In the first, the Fool wins, because there is no Pagat. In the second, 4♥︎ wins. A♦︎ is a Pagat, but without a Fool in the trick it is just the lowest tarock.

6Card points

At the end of a deal, you count the cards you won. Each card is worth some points.

CardCard points
Each Honour (Fool, Mond, Pagat)4
King4
Queen3
Jack2
101
Any other card0
Each trick you won1

With three players, a deal has 16 tricks and 80 card points in all.

Check yourself. You win a trick with K♠︎, 10♠︎ and A♥︎ in it. How many card points is it worth?

4 for the King, 1 for the 10, 4 for the Pagat and 1 for the trick. That makes 10.

Try it now. Play one practice deal with steps 0 to 6. Put 6 cards face down to one side and deal 16 to each player. Forehand leads. Skip the bidding and the chips. Each player keeps their own pile, and at the end you count your card points. The player with the most wins the practice deal.

Part 2 Bidding and chips

7The bidding

The 6 cards set aside are the talon. Before play, you bid for some of them. Whoever wins the bidding plays alone against the other two. The fewer talon cards you ask for, the higher your bid, and the more it is worth.

BidTalon cardsBid value
Three32
Two24
One16
Zeronone10

Forehand speaks first, then clockwise. On your turn, bid higher than the last bid, or pass. Once you pass, you are out of the bidding for this deal. When two players have passed, the third wins with their last bid. If all three pass, you play Ramsch (step 13).

The words The player who wins the bidding is the Declarer. The other two are the Defenders, and they play together.

Check yourself. A is Forehand. A bids Three, B bids Two, C passes. A bids One, and B passes. Who is the Declarer?

A, with One. B and C have both passed.

8The talon

After a bid of Three, Two or One, turn the 6 talon cards face up without changing their order. They form groups in a row.

  • Three gives two groups of 3 cards.
  • Two gives three groups of 2 cards.
  • One gives six single cards.

After a bid of Two, the talon forms three groups.

Group 1K♥︎3♠︎
Group 2JK8♦︎
Group 3A♣︎Q♣︎

The Declarer takes one group into their hand. Then they put the same number of cards from their hand onto their own pile. Those cards count for the Declarer at the end. Kings and Honours may not be put away, and tarocks put away are shown face up. The other talon cards go to the Defenders’ pile.

After a bid of Zero, nobody sees the talon. It goes straight to the Defenders’ pile.

The word Putting cards away is discarding.

9The Point game

Usually the Declarer now plays a Point game. They need at least 41 of the 80 card points. The Defenders share one pile and try to stop them.

Forehand leads the first trick, and play goes as in your practice deal.

Check yourself. The Declarer ends with 40 card points. Did they win?

No. They need at least 41. With 40, the Defenders have the other 40, and the Declarer loses.

10Moments pay at once

In a Point game, some tricks pay chips at once. Whoever wins the trick says what it holds, and the players who owe pay right away. Chips only go between opponents, so between the Declarer and a Defender. Defenders never pay each other.

Queen’s Ransom
A King and the Queen of its suit are in the same trick. The Queen’s owner pays the winner 1 chip.
Fallen King
You lead a King, and another player wins the trick. You pay the winner 1 chip.
Honour Capture
An Honour wins the trick. The owner of each other Honour in it pays the winner 2 chips. A Pagat captures a Fool, a Fool a Mond, a Mond a Pagat, and a twin can capture its twin.
Pagat Ultimo
A Pagat in the last trick, the 16th. If it wins, a Defender collects 2 chips from the Declarer, or the Declarer collects 1 from each Defender. If it loses, its owner pays the winner 2 chips, if they are opponents. A Pagat still in your hand at the end is always played to the last trick.

For example, B leads K♥︎ and C plays Q♥︎. You are the Declarer, you have no hearts, and you play 3♣︎.

K♥︎Q♥︎3♣︎

You win. B pays you 1 for the Fallen King, and C pays you 1 for the Queen’s Ransom.

The word These special tricks are moments.

Check yourself. You are a Defender. Your fellow Defender wins a trick in which you played a Queen next to its King. Do you pay?

No. Moments only pay between opponents, and the two Defenders are on the same side.

11Settling the deal

After the last trick, count the Declarer’s card points. That is everything on their pile, including the cards they put away, plus 1 for each trick they won. The Defenders have the rest of the 80.

The deal is worth the bid value (step 7) plus a margin for how clearly it was won or lost.

Declarer’s card pointsMargin
Won with 41 to 46, or lost with 35 to 400
Won with 47 to 52, or lost with 29 to 341
Won with 53 to 58, or lost with 23 to 282
Won with 59 to 64, or lost with 17 to 223
Won with 65 or more, or lost with 0 to 164

If the Declarer won, each Defender pays them that many chips. If the Declarer lost, they pay that many to each Defender.

For example, A plays Two and takes 53 card points. The margin is 2, so the deal is worth 4 + 2 = 6. B and C each pay A 6 chips.

Check yourself. You bid Three and end with 38 card points. What happens?

You lost, with a margin of 0. The deal is worth 2 + 0 = 2, so you pay 2 chips to each Defender.

12Tightrope

After a bid of One or Zero, the Declarer may play Tightrope instead of a Point game. Then they must win at most one trick. The Declarer leads the first trick. As soon as they win a second trick, they lose, and the deal ends. Tightrope is worth the bid value plus 2.

In Tightrope, two extra rules apply to every player.

  1. You must beat if you can. If one of the cards you may play would win the trick as it stands, you must play such a card. A twin never beats its twin.
  2. Pagats wait for a Fool. You may only play a Pagat when a Fool is in the trick and no Pagat yet, or when the cards you may play are all Pagats. This applies when you lead, too.

The word These two are the negative rules.

There are no moments in Tightrope.

Check yourself. In Tightrope, J♦︎ is led and you hold Q♦︎ and 7♦︎. Which may you play?

Only Q♦︎. It would win the trick as it stands, so the first negative rule makes you play it.

Check yourself. In Tightrope, 9♣︎ is led. You have no clubs, and your only tarocks are A♥︎ and 3♦︎. Which may you play?

Only 3♦︎. You must play a tarock, and both would win the trick as it stands. But A♥︎ is a Pagat, and there is no Fool in the trick, so it has to wait.

13Ramsch, when nobody bids

If all three players pass, you play Ramsch. Everyone plays alone and tries to take as few card points as possible. The negative rules from step 12 apply, and there are no moments.

The talon stays face down in the middle. The winner of each of the first 6 tricks also takes the top talon card, shows it and adds it to their pile. It counts its card points, but it isn’t a trick.

At the end, everyone counts their own card points. The player with the most pays each other player 3 chips. If two players tie for the most, each of them pays the third player 3 chips.

Check yourself. A ends with 36 card points, B with 30 and C with 14. Who pays?

A has the most, so A pays 3 chips to B and 3 to C.

14Winning the match

Everyone starts with 50 chips. The other chips stay in the box as the bank. After each deal, the player to the dealer’s left deals next.

A match is 3 rounds. In each round every player deals once, so a match has 9 deals. Then the player with the most chips wins. If players tie, they share the win.

★A deal from the bid to the payment

The bidding

You are Forehand, and your hand has many tarocks, among them a Fool. You bid Two, and B and C pass. You are the Declarer, and you play a Point game against B and C.

The talon

You turn the talon face up. It forms three groups, as in step 8.

Group 1K♥︎3♠︎
Group 2JK8♦︎
Group 3A♣︎Q♣︎

You take Group 3. The Mond A♣︎ is a strong tarock worth 4 card points, and Q♣︎ is worth 3.

The discard

You put J♦︎ and 10♦︎ face down on your pile. They count for you at the end, so these 3 card points are safe. The other four talon cards go to the Defenders’ pile, so they start with 4 + 0 + 4 + 0 = 8 card points.

The Juggler’s Trick

Later, you lead your Fool to draw out tarocks. B plays 5♣︎, and C plays the Pagat A♥︎.

JK5♣︎A♥︎

The Pagat beats the Fool, so C wins. Their Pagat captures your Fool, and you pay C 2 chips. The trick is worth 4 + 0 + 4 + 1 = 9 card points for the Defenders.

A Mond captures a Pagat

C won, so C leads next, 4♠︎. You play your Mond A♣︎. B has no other tarock left, so B must play the Pagat A♦︎.

4♠︎A♣︎A♦︎

You win. Your Mond captures B’s Pagat, so B pays you 2 chips. The trick is worth 0 + 4 + 4 + 1 = 9 card points.

The end of the deal

After the 16th trick, you count your pile, with J♦︎ and 10♦︎. You have 53 card points. Two is worth 4, plus a margin of 2, so B and C each pay you 6 chips.

The result

You won 2 chips and lost 2 during play, then won 12 at the end. If nothing else happened, you are 12 chips up.

Part 3 Playing with four

15What changes with four

With four players, everything from Parts 1 and 2 still holds, except for these changes.

  • Deal 12 cards to each player, in packets of 6, after setting aside the 6 talon cards.
  • The bidding ends when three players have passed.
  • A deal has 12 tricks and 76 card points. The Declarer’s side needs 39.
  • There is no Tightrope. The negative rules only apply in Ramsch.
  • The talon cards the Declarer doesn’t take are set aside face down. They count for the Defenders at the end.
  • Every player keeps their own pile. At the end, each side puts its piles together.
  • A match is 2 or 3 rounds, so 8 or 12 deals. Agree on it before you start.
Card points of the Declarer’s sideMargin
Won with 39 to 44, or lost with 33 to 380
Won with 45 to 50, or lost with 27 to 321
Won with 51 to 56, or lost with 21 to 262
Won with 57 to 62, or lost with 15 to 203
Won with 63 or more, or lost with 0 to 144

16Calling a King

After the talon, the Declarer names one King, for example “the King of hearts”. Whoever holds that King becomes the Declarer’s secret partner, but says nothing. The other two are the Defenders. So it’s two against two, but at first nobody knows who plays with whom.

The sides become known when the called King is played. If it was among the face-up talon cards, they are known at once, even if the Declarer took it.

Playing alone. If the Declarer calls a King they hold themselves, they play alone against all three, and nobody knows it until that King is played. Calling a King from the talon also means playing alone. If you hold all four Kings, you must call one of them.

After a bid of Zero, nobody sees the talon, so the called King may lie in it. Then the Declarer plays alone, and nobody knows until the end of the deal. In return, the talon counts for the Declarer.

Check yourself. You are the Declarer and hold K♠︎. You call the King of spades. Who is your partner?

Nobody. You called your own King, so you play alone against the other three, and they don’t know it yet.

17The exchange

When the called King is played and it is two against two, the partners swap one card after that trick. Each player chooses a card from their hand and passes it face down to their partner, all four at the same time. Then the winner of the trick leads as usual.

There is no exchange if the called King is played in the last trick, the 12th.

Check yourself. You are the Declarer and call the King of spades, which you hold yourself. You play it in the 4th trick. Is there an exchange?

No. You play alone, one against three, so there are no partners to swap cards. The exchange only happens when it is two against two.

18Chips with hidden sides

Moments only pay between opponents, but while the sides are hidden, nobody knows who those are. So payments wait. Whoever owes puts the chips in front of them, apart from their stack, pointing toward the trick’s winner. Once the sides are known, hand the waiting chips to opponents and take back the rest. If the sides stay hidden, sort them out at the end of the deal.

The trick in which the called King is played already pays directly.

The word Chips waiting like this are held.

For example, A is the Declarer and calls the King of hearts. B leads K♣︎, C plays Q♣︎, D trumps with 3♠︎, and A plays 7♣︎. D wins.

K♣︎Q♣︎3♠︎7♣︎

For the Fallen King, B puts 1 chip in front of them toward D. For the Queen’s Ransom, C does the same. Later, C plays K♥︎, so C is A’s partner, and B and D are the Defenders. C hands their chip to D, and B takes theirs back.

Check yourself. In this example, what if D had played K♥︎ instead, so D is A’s partner?

Then B and C are the Defenders, and D is an opponent of both. So B and C each hand their chip to D.

Pagat Ultimo is never held. If the sides are still hidden, pay it at the end of the deal. If a Pagat wins the 12th trick, each opponent of the winner pays them 1 chip, and a lone Declarer pays 3 instead. If it loses, its owner pays the winner 2 chips, as with three players.

Settling. If the Declarer has a partner, each Defender settles the deal’s value with one player of the Declarer’s side. If the Declarer plays alone, they settle with each of the three Defenders.

For example, A plays Two with C as partner, and together they take 51 card points. The deal is worth 4 + 2 = 6. B pays A 6 chips, and D pays C 6 chips. Had A played alone, B, C and D would each have paid A 6 chips.

Ramsch with four. Each player pays 2 chips to every player with fewer card points. Players with equal card points pay each other nothing.

Check yourself. In Ramsch, A takes 30 card points, B 24, C 14 and D 8. How much does each player win or lose?

A pays 2 each to B, C and D. B pays 2 each to C and D, and C pays 2 to D. So A loses 6, B loses 2, C wins 2, and D wins 6.

You now know the whole game. Part 4 has smaller rules. They matter in play, but you can look them up when they come up.

Part 4 More details

Small rules

Dealing

Choose the first dealer at random. The dealer shuffles, and the player to the dealer’s right cuts. The dealer puts the top 6 cards face down as the talon, then deals packets of 8 cards (with four players, 6 cards), starting with Forehand.

Before the bidding, a player who has no tarocks shows their hand, and the same dealer deals again.

Calling a missed moment

Until the next card is led, any player may still call a moment that was missed. After the last trick, this is possible until counting begins.

Counting quickly

Count the smaller pile. Pull out the Honours, Kings, Queens, Jacks and 10s, add up their card points, and add 1 for each trick. If it is the Defenders’ pile, subtract the total from 80, or from 76 with four players.

Running out of chips

If you have to pay more chips than you have, borrow 10 from the bank, as often as you need, and note it. If the bank runs out, note further loans on paper. At the end of the match, subtract your loans. Totals may be below 0.

Keeping score on paper

Write one column per player. Everyone starts at 0, and scores may go below 0. Each chip is one point. During the deal, note each moment payment, for example “A +1, B −1”. After the deal, add the payment for the deal and write each player’s change in one row. Every row adds up to 0, which shows a mistake at once.

With four players, circle the entries made while the sides are hidden. Once they are known, cross out circled entries between players on the same side.

The board

The rules PDF has a board to lay in the middle of the table as a play aid. A coin on it marks the contract. You don’t need it to play.

Tips for your first matches

  • Count your tarocks before you bid. They win most tricks.
  • Think twice before leading a King. If an opponent can beat it, it costs you a chip.
  • Keep a Pagat while a Fool is still out. In a Point game, catching a Fool from an opponent pays 2 chips.
  • With four players, watch who plays which King. Until the called King shows up, anyone could be your partner.
